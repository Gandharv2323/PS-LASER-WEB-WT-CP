Abstract—In the sheet-metal job shop studied here, quotation requests arrive by telephone and WhatsApp, which delays pricing, scatters drawing files and gives customers no view of job progress. This paper presents PS Laser, a web platform built for a laser-cutting job shop at Velu, Bhor (Pune). It provides a public website, an instant indicative cost estimator, an enquiry and drawing-upload service, order tracking and a staff back office. The platform is implemented as seven cooperating services (PHP, Laravel, React with Redux, Servlet/JSP, Spring Boot, Next.js with TypeScript, and Node.js with Express) that share one MySQL schema and one written contract covering the price formula, JWT authentication and API shapes. The same estimate formula, with one rounding rule, is implemented in four languages and returned the same total (INR 2,933.83) for a reference part on all four stacks. We describe the architecture, the cost model, the security measures (JWT, bcrypt, CSRF tokens, prepared statements, upload validation, rate limiting) and a push channel for live work-order status. Verification consists of cross-stack equivalence checks, endpoint and validation tests, and automated suites (33 Node.js tests and 8 Laravel tests passing on 18 October 2026). The rate card is sample data and the system is neither deployed nor connected to shop-floor machines; these limits and future work are discussed.

Index Terms—web technologies, cost estimation, laser cutting, sheet-metal fabrication, REST API, JWT, Spring Boot, Next.js, React, Laravel, MySQL, order tracking

I. Introduction

PS Laser is a sheet-metal and CNC laser-cutting job shop at Velu, Tal. Bhor, Pune. Its equipment comprises two fiber lasers (3 kW and 1.5 kW, 3 m × 1.5 m beds), a 175 t CNC press brake, two Amada turret punches (32 t and 16 t), a 1.5 kW handheld laser welder and a PLC-controlled powder-coating plant. The shop's published customer channels are the telephone and WhatsApp: a customer describes a part, sends a drawing, and waits for a manual quotation.

This channel has three practical weaknesses. First, a customer cannot get even an approximate price without waiting for a person. Second, drawings and requirements arrive as chat attachments that are hard to search, track or audit. Third, once a job is accepted the customer has no way to see whether it is being cut, bent, coated or ready for dispatch. These weaknesses are specific to the shop we studied; we do not claim they hold for every fabricator.

The platform described here addresses them with a mobile-friendly website, an indicative estimator, an enquiry form with drawing upload, a phone-number-verified order-tracking page and staff screens for managing enquiries, the rate card and work orders. The work was also carried out as the course project of CS3111 Web Technologies, whose six units cover PHP/Laravel, React, Servlets/JSP, Spring Boot, advanced React and Node.js. We therefore implemented one real feature per technology instead of a single monolith, and spent most of the design effort on keeping those independent services consistent with each other.

The contributions of this paper are: (i) a contract-first architecture in which seven services share one database schema, one JWT secret and one price formula; (ii) a rate-card cost model that gives identical results in PHP, Java, Spring Boot and TypeScript; (iii) a security and resilience design that includes a graceful offline fallback for the public site; and (iv) an honest account of what was verified, what was not, and which values are sample data.

II. Problem Statement and Objectives

Problem statement. Design and build a web platform that lets a laser-cutting job shop (a) present its real machines and services, (b) give customers an instant, clearly labelled indicative price, (c) capture enquiries and drawings reliably, (d) let customers follow a job to dispatch, and (e) let staff maintain prices and job status without editing the database by hand.

Objectives.

  1. Provide a public website with the shop's machines, services and contact channels, with every "Get Quote" action opening a pre-filled WhatsApp message.
  2. Compute an indicative estimate from material, thickness, size, cut length, pierces, bends, optional powder coating and quantity, using a rate card that the owner can change.
  3. Store enquiries and drawing files (DXF, DWG, PDF, PNG, JPG, up to 10 MB) with server-side validation.
  4. Expose order tracking that requires the order number and the customer's phone number, and a staff dashboard that updates live.
  5. Keep all services consistent through one database, one authentication token format and one documented API contract.
  6. Make the platform demonstrate every unit of the CS3111 syllabus with working code.

III. Background and Related Work

The platform relies on well-established web-engineering ideas. The REST architectural style [1] guides the Spring Boot and Node.js APIs, and the model-view-controller separation [2] organises the Laravel, Servlet/JSP and Spring code. Stateless authentication uses JSON Web Tokens [3] signed with HMAC-SHA-256 (the HS256 algorithm defined in [4]); passwords are stored as adaptive bcrypt hashes [5]. Live status updates use the WebSocket protocol [6] with a STOMP broker in Spring. The web-application risks that shaped the validation and access-control design are those catalogued by OWASP [7], and the quality attributes used in the requirements document follow ISO/IEC 25010 [8].

On the manufacturing side, laser-processing texts such as [9] describe the process parameters (laser power, material, thickness and cutting speed) that determine cut quality and throughput. Our estimator deliberately does not model that physics. It is a rate-card model, in which each cost component is a quantity multiplied by an owner-set rate, because a shop needs a quick, explainable and editable price indication rather than a process simulation.

Scope of the literature review. We did not identify directly comparable, verifiable publications on small-shop quotation portals and therefore make no novelty claim about the estimator itself. The contribution lies in the integration and consistency of the multi-stack design. A systematic comparison with commercial online-quotation tools is left as future work.

IV. Proposed Methodology and System Design

A. Contract-first, shared-schema approach

Before any service was written, a contract document (services/CONTRACT.md) fixed the database name and tables, the JWT claims and secret, the estimate formula and its JSON shape, the REST endpoints of the Spring and Node services, and the allowed CORS origins. Each service was then built against that document. The deliberate trade-off is that PHP, Laravel and the Java servlet application read and write MySQL directly instead of calling the Spring API. This reduces the number of network hops and lets each syllabus unit stand alone, but it duplicates some business rules, which is why the price formula is specified once and tested in every stack.

B. Architecture

Fig. 1 shows the architecture as built. The client tier is a browser used by customers and staff. The presentation tier has five front ends: the Next.js website (port 3001), the React portal (5173), the PHP estimator (8000), the Laravel admin panel (8001) and the Servlet/JSP desk on Tomcat (8080). The application tier consists of the Spring Boot REST API (8081) and the Node.js/Express API (4000). The data tier is MySQL/MariaDB (3306) with MongoDB (27017) used only for an audit activity log. The physical shop is shown for context only: no machine is connected to the software, and staff update job status by hand.

C. Data model

One MySQL database, pslaser, holds eleven shared tables: users, machines, services, materials, rates, settings, enquiries, uploads, quotes, work_orders and work_order_events, plus framework-owned tables created by Laravel. Rates are stored per material and thickness band, and a settings row marks the rate card as sample data. A work order moves through seven stages: received, cutting, bending, welding, coating, inspection and dispatched. Every change appends a row to work_order_events, which is what the customer sees as a timeline.

D. Cost model

Inputs are the material code, thickness t (mm), part length L and width W (mm), cut length ℓ (mm), pierces np, bends nb, a powder-coat flag and quantity q. After validation (the material exists, 0 < t ≤ its maximum, quantity ≥ 1, and the part fits the 3000 × 1500 mm bed), the rate row whose thickness band contains t is selected. The estimate is:

A = L·W / 106 [m²], m = A·t·ρ [kg](1)
Cmat = m·rkg, Ccut = (ℓ/1000)·rcut, Cprc = np·rp, Cbnd = nb·rb(2)
Ccoat = 2·A·rc (powder coat only, otherwise 0)(3)
P = Σ round2(Ci), S = round2(P·q), G = round2(S·g/100), T = S + G(4)

Here ρ is the material density in g/cm³ (so A·t·ρ is in kg), rkg, rcut and rp come from the selected rate row, rb and rc are the bend and powder-coat rates, g is the GST percentage (18 % in the seed data), and round2 rounds half-up to two decimals. The rule "round each line, then add" is the single rule that keeps the four implementations identical to the paisa. The seed rate card is sample data (flag is_sample = 1), so every estimate is labelled "Indicative estimate; final quote on WhatsApp".

E. Security design

  • Authentication. Spring Boot issues an HS256 JWT (8 h) that Node.js also accepts because both services share one secret. Roles are admin, staff and customer. The Next.js site keeps the token in an httpOnly, SameSite=Lax cookie that browser JavaScript cannot read.
  • Passwords. Stored as bcrypt ($2y$) hashes in the shared users table.
  • Input handling. Node.js validates bodies with zod and uses prepared statements; PHP and Laravel use CSRF tokens (a missing token yields HTTP 303 or 419); the estimate API rejects out-of-range inputs with HTTP 400 and a field-level error body.
  • Uploads. Allowed types are DXF, DWG, PDF, PNG and JPG up to 10 MB; files are stored privately and served only to authenticated staff.
  • Abuse control. The enquiry endpoint is rate-limited (HTTP 429), Node.js uses the helmet header set, and all APIs accept only an allow-list of origins.
  • Privacy in tracking. An order is shown only if the order number and phone number both match, and the phone number is displayed masked.

F. Live updates and offline resilience

When staff change a job status, Spring Boot saves the change and an event row, then publishes a message on the STOMP topic /topic/work-orders that the Next.js staff dashboard subscribes to. The public Next.js estimator calls Spring through its own route handler; if Spring is unreachable, the same formula is computed in TypeScript and the page shows "Offline estimate (sample)". The React portal similarly falls back to demo data and says so.

We report only checks that were actually executed. Dates are those on which a check was run; the longer QA run was on 14 October 2026 and the re-runs below on 16 and 18 October 2026.

C. Not verified

The following were not tested, and we make no claim about them: responsive layout at 375, 768 and 1440 px by visual inspection; every image on every page; the end-to-end path from the React portal through Node.js to the database; delivery of a live STOMP push to the dashboard; re-testing of upload rejection with real files; any load or performance measurement; and any deployment outside a single Windows PC. In particular, no latency or throughput figures are reported because none were measured.

VII. Discussion and Limitations

  • Sample prices. The rate card is placeholder data. Estimates must not be used to quote customers until the owner enters real rates and clears the sample flag.
  • Duplicated rules. Because three applications use the database directly, a rule change (for example in rounding) must be made in four places. The shared contract and the reference test case mitigate this but do not remove it.
  • No machine integration. Job status is entered by staff; there is no connection to the lasers, press brake or punches, and no CAD/DXF analysis. Cut length and pierce count are typed by the user.
  • Delivery channels. Enquiries produce a database row and a WhatsApp link; there is no e-mail or SMS service and no payment.
  • Deployment and secrets. The system runs locally with development defaults (database root user with empty password, development JWT secret). These must be replaced before any public deployment, which would also need HTTPS and backups.
  • Data durability. The embedded MongoDB instance loses data on restart.
  • Evidence base. Results come from one development machine and a small seeded data set; they demonstrate correctness and consistency, not scalability.

VIII. Conclusion and Future Work

We designed and implemented PS Laser, a platform that gives a laser-cutting job shop an instant indicative estimator, enquiry and drawing capture, order tracking and staff tools, built as seven cooperating services over one shared schema and contract. The reference estimate matched across four independent implementations, and the automated suites that we re-ran (Node.js and Laravel) passed. Future work is to replace the sample rate card with the owner's real rates, add e-mail or WhatsApp Business notifications on status change, deploy behind HTTPS with managed MySQL and secrets, add end-to-end browser tests, parse DXF files to propose cut length and pierces automatically, and evaluate the estimator against the shop's actual quotations.
